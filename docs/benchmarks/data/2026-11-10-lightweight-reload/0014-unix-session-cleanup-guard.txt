## Context

ADR 0013 centralizes configuration decisions and native ownership. On Unix,
private process groups provide signalling boundaries but do not automatically
terminate when their owner dies. A worker-side lifetime watcher disappears if
that worker crashes first, leaving no owner to terminate its surviving helpers
after the configuration supervisor also dies. The
[native investigation](../benchmarks/2026-10-24-supervisor-native-lifetime.md)
reproduces this failure order on Linux.

Blocking replacement without cleanup proof remains safe, but losing speech until
manual intervention is a substantial availability cost. Native ownership needs
to outlive configuration-supervisor failure without restoring per-lane owners or
distributed configuration decisions.

## Decision

Use one dedicated native cleanup guard process for the whole private Unix
session, serving its bounded collection of isolated lane workers. The guard owns
worker creation, native launch gates, live child/process-group identities and
tree retirement. Establish ownership before any worker initializes engines or
helpers. Keep the group leader unreaped until the group's termination signal;
saved numerical PIDs never grant a successor signalling authority.

The configuration supervisor owns membership, prepared startup/recovery records,
asset retention, immutable Commit/Abort decisions and terminal configuration
results. The guard neither selects configuration nor votes in reload. Native
ownership and lifecycle observations use a private bounded channel, separate from
speech and client output. The guard must continue cleanup when those outputs or
worker inputs are blocked. Lifetime descriptors must not leak into workers or
helpers and keep a dead supervisor apparently alive.

Loss of the configuration supervisor closes admission for the entire session
and causes the guard to retire all owned trees, including gated workers and
helpers whose worker has already exited. Keep per-lane retirement possible while
the supervisor survives; a failed lane cannot prevent attempts to retire others.
Guard shutdown and startup failure retain the same ownership obligations.

Guard failure, unavailable native evidence or incomplete retirement keeps
recovery blocked. Do not infer cleanup from guard exit, a timeout, a free lock,
EOF or a PID lookup. Durable cleanup evidence must bind the exact session, guard
and worker incarnations and become available before a successor releases retained
assets or starts replacement workers. Native tree cleanup and completion of any
speech I/O tasks are distinct obligations. Uncertain or damaged records retain
asset pins and conflicting-management exclusion.

Supervisor loss still ends the session: successors do not attach to surviving
workers. Reconcile saved decisions and prove cleanup before starting fresh workers
from the last conclusively committed configuration. Do not reread edited files or
replay interrupted speech. Before enabling the path, provide explicit unavailable
status and a documented, tested recovery procedure; repeated restarts must not
silently discard blocked ownership.

Windows may keep its existing private kill-on-close job boundary, subject to
native qualification of the new session path. Linux and macOS need their own
cleanup evidence. This decision does not introduce a shared daemon, engine pool,
remote management, helper/output Apply, a required external service manager or a
new distributable helper. The guard can run as a private mode of the selected
Omnivox executable. Existing standalone, local managed and remote paths retain
their contracts until an explicitly negotiated replacement is qualified.

## Consequences and alternatives

The extra process retains native authority after configuration-supervisor loss
and serves every lane. It adds a lifecycle channel, startup ordering and durable
cleanup accounting, while configuration coordination remains centralized. It
does not guarantee recovery after simultaneous guard failure or eliminate
per-lane model memory. Process groups assume qualified helpers remain in their
owned group; they are not a sandbox for arbitrary hostile executables.

Keeping all ownership in one supervisor avoids the extra process but leaves the
demonstrated failures blocked. A worker watcher alone cannot cover prior worker
death. Externally managed kernel containment can strengthen Linux ownership but
adds deployment requirements and does not supply the macOS solution. Per-lane
owners retain more processes and coordination than the chosen session-wide guard.
